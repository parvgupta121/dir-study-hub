01What Is Anthropology?
CHAPTER 1 — What Is Anthropology?
  Scope in your syllabus: Full chapter

  Anthropology is the comparative and holistic study of humans, their biology, cultures, languages, societies and histories. It asks
  what humans share, how they differ and how those differences developed.

  Anthropology
  Meaning: The systematic study of humans across time and place, including biological, cultural, linguistic and archaeological
  dimensions.
    Example 1: Comparing marriage practices in several societies.
    Why it fits: The comparison explains variation instead of assuming one form of marriage is universal.
    Example 2: Studying how people in different environments obtain food.
    Why it fits: Food-getting links human behavior with ecology, technology, social organization and culture.

  Application / exam connection: Use anthropology to explain a practice in context rather than judging it by your own
  expectations.

  Holism
  Meaning: A perspective that understands human life by examining interconnected dimensions rather than isolating one factor.
    Example 1: Studying school performance through family resources, language, nutrition, peer groups and school policies.
    Why it fits: It combines social, cultural, economic and biological influences.
    Example 2: Studying workplace behavior through hierarchy, communication, incentives and shared values.
    Why it fits: It treats organizational behavior as the outcome of connected factors.

  Four major fields
  Meaning: Biological anthropology studies human biology and evolution; cultural anthropology studies learned ways of life;
  archaeology studies past societies through material remains; linguistic anthropology studies language in social and cultural
  context.
    Example 1: Analysis of ancient human bones and adaptation.
    Why it fits: This is biological anthropology because it studies biological evidence and evolution.
    Example 2: Study of how a community uses language differently at home and in public.
    Why it fits: This is linguistic anthropology because it examines language in social context.

  Application / exam connection: Fields overlap: for example, archaeological objects may reveal both technology and cultural
  meaning.

  Cultural anthropology
  Meaning: The study of learned and shared patterns of behavior, ideas, values, institutions and meanings in human groups.
    Example 1: Rules about greeting elders.
    Why it fits: They are learned social expectations and communicate respect.
    Example 2: Food restrictions connected to religious or community identity.
    Why it fits: They are culturally meaningful rules, not simply biological preferences.

  Applied anthropology
  Meaning: Using anthropological concepts and methods to address practical problems.
    Example 1: Researching how patients understand a public-health message before redesigning it.
    Why it fits: The research uses cultural understanding to improve a real intervention.
    Example 2: Observing how customers use a product in their homes before redesigning it.
    Why it fits: Ethnographic insight informs practical product decisions.

  Ethnography
  Meaning: A detailed account of a community or social setting, usually based on sustained fieldwork and observation.
    Example 1: A researcher spends months observing work and family routines in a fishing village.
    Why it fits: The account is grounded in extended, contextual fieldwork.
    Example 2: A researcher documents everyday interaction in a hospital ward.
    Why it fits: The study describes actual practices in their setting rather than relying only on assumptions.

  Ethnology / cross-cultural comparison
  Meaning: The systematic comparison of cultural patterns across societies to identify similarities, differences and possible
  explanations.
    Example 1: Comparing inheritance customs across communities.
    Why it fits: It identifies patterned variation across cultures.
    Example 2: Comparing child-care practices in rural and urban settings.

02Culture and Culture Change
CHAPTER 2 — Culture and Culture Change
  Scope in your syllabus: Full chapter

  Culture is learned, shared and patterned. It includes ideas and practices, material objects and the meanings people attach to
  them. Culture is dynamic, not fixed.

  Culture
  Meaning: A learned and shared system of ideas, values, norms, symbols, practices and material objects through which people
  organize life.
    Example 1: Language learned in childhood.
    Why it fits: A language is socially acquired and shared with other speakers.
    Example 2: Rules about what counts as polite behavior at a meal.
    Why it fits: The rules are learned, shared and meaningful within a group.

  Culture is learned
  Meaning: Cultural knowledge is acquired through socialization and experience rather than inherited genetically.
    Example 1: Children learn how to greet guests by watching adults.
    Why it fits: The behavior is acquired through observation and teaching.
    Example 2: A student learns classroom turn-taking rules.
    Why it fits: The rules are learned through participation in an institution.

  Culture is shared but not identical
  Meaning: Members of a group recognize common patterns, although individuals interpret or follow them differently.
    Example 1: A community celebrates a festival using shared symbols and routines.
    Why it fits: Common practices create collective meaning even when families celebrate differently.
    Example 2: Employees share expectations about punctuality.
    Why it fits: A shared workplace norm exists even if individual punctuality varies.

  Culture is integrated / patterned
  Meaning: Cultural elements are connected; changes in one area can influence other areas.
    Example 1: A value placed on formal education shapes family investment, school attendance and career expectations.
    Why it fits: Several practices connect around a shared value.
    Example 2: Ideas about hospitality influence food, guest seating and gift-giving.
    Why it fits: Different customs express and reinforce a related cultural pattern.

  Cultural relativism
  Meaning: Understanding a practice in its own cultural and historical context rather than judging it only by one's own standards.
    Example 1: Examining why a community follows a particular marriage custom by studying kinship, economy and local meanings.
    Why it fits: The practice is explained in context before evaluation.
    Example 2: Understanding food avoidance through local religion or ecology.
    Why it fits: The explanation uses the community's meanings rather than an outsider's preference.

  Application / exam connection: Cultural relativism is a method of understanding; it does not require approving every practice.

  Ethnocentrism
  Meaning: Judging another culture using one's own culture as the standard, often treating one's own practices as normal or
  superior.
    Example 1: Calling an unfamiliar greeting rude because it differs from one's own greeting.
    Why it fits: The judgment uses the observer's norm as the universal standard.
    Example 2: Assuming one's own cuisine is normal and other food traditions are strange.
    Why it fits: The reaction reflects cultural bias rather than contextual understanding.

  Diffusion
  Meaning: The spread of cultural traits between groups through contact, trade, migration, media or other connections.
    Example 1: A musical style spreads internationally and is adapted by local performers.
    Why it fits: A cultural form moves between groups.
    Example 2: A food ingredient travels through trade and becomes part of regional cuisine.
    Why it fits: The ingredient spreads through contact and is locally incorporated.

  Acculturation
  Meaning: Cultural change resulting from sustained contact between groups; one or both groups may adopt or adapt traits.
    Example 1: A migrant family learns the dominant language while retaining its home language.
    Why it fits: Sustained contact leads to selective cultural adaptation.
    Example 2: A local restaurant adapts an international dish to local tastes.
    Why it fits: The imported practice changes through contact with local preferences.

  Globalization
  Meaning: Increasing interconnectedness through movement of people, goods, money, information and ideas across regions and
  countries.
    Example 1: A global clothing brand changes products for different local markets.
    Why it fits: Global business is adapted to local cultural contexts.
    Example 2: Social-media trends circulate across countries within days.
    Why it fits: Digital networks intensify cross-border cultural exchange.

  Cultural change: innovation and invention
  Meaning: New ideas or practices emerge and may become part of a culture.
    Example 1: A community develops a new water-storage practice in response to drought.
    Why it fits: A new solution changes everyday behavior.
    Example 2: A new digital payment method changes market transactions.
    Why it fits: Technological innovation alters economic routines.

  Cultural change: resistance and revival
  Meaning: Groups may resist outside pressures or deliberately revive practices that support identity.
    Example 1: A community creates classes to teach a threatened language to children.
    Why it fits: The community actively maintains cultural knowledge.
    Example 2: Artisans revive a traditional weaving technique for contemporary products.
    Why it fits: A practice is consciously renewed and adapted.
    Government and culture change: Government policy can influence institutions and public behavior, but people interpret, negotiate
    and sometimes resist policy. Change is rarely fully controlled from above.
    Development and local culture: Development projects can fail when they ignore local knowledge, livelihood patterns and social
    organization. Consultation and participation help align projects with community needs.
    Climate change and culture change: Changing rainfall, temperature and resource availability can alter food production, mobility,
    settlement and livelihood strategies. Responses differ according to resources and institutions.
    Cultural diversity in the future: Global connections may encourage borrowing and shared popular culture while communities also
    preserve or reinvent local identities.
03Culture and the Individual
CHAPTER 3 — Culture and the Individual
  Scope in your syllabus: Sections 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3

  This chapter considers how culture and individual development influence each other. Human beings share developmental
  capacities, but social expectations shape how they are expressed.

  Enculturation
  Meaning: The process through which a person learns the culture of their group.
    Example 1: A child learns the language spoken at home.
    Why it fits: The child acquires shared cultural knowledge through social interaction.
    Example 2: A young person learns how to behave during a community ceremony.
    Why it fits: The person learns culturally appropriate conduct by participating.

  Socialization
  Meaning: The lifelong process of learning social roles, norms, values and expected behavior.
    Example 1: A student learns how to participate in group discussions.
    Why it fits: The student acquires role expectations in an educational setting.
    Example 2: A new employee learns workplace communication norms.
    Why it fits: The employee learns the behavior expected in that social institution.

  Culture and personality
  Meaning: Culture influences the values, habits and behavioral expectations people develop, while individuals also interpret and
  shape culture.
    Example 1: Children in one setting may be encouraged to speak confidently in public.
    Why it fits: Social expectations help shape communication style.
    Example 2: Children in another setting may be encouraged to listen carefully before speaking.
    Why it fits: Different expectations can shape interaction patterns.

  Child-rearing variation
  Meaning: Caregiving, discipline, independence and responsibilities vary across societies and households.
    Example 1: Infants are carried close to caregivers during daily work.
    Why it fits: This illustrates a culturally organized caregiving pattern.
    Example 2: Children are expected to help with household tasks at different ages.
    Why it fits: The timing and meaning of responsibility reflect local expectations.

  Emotional expression
  Meaning: People experience emotions, but cultural rules influence when and how emotions are displayed.
    Example 1: A community encourages restrained public grief.
    Why it fits: The display rule shapes expression, not necessarily the underlying feeling.
    Example 2: A family encourages open celebration and expressive joy.
    Why it fits: Social norms support visible emotional expression.

  Development and learning
  Meaning: Human development has shared biological foundations, but learning environments shape skills, attention and
  behavior.
    Example 1: Children learn local ecological knowledge by accompanying adults.
    Why it fits: Development is shaped by participation in everyday activities.
    Example 2: Children learn formal literacy through school.
    Why it fits: Institutional learning shapes particular skills and competencies.
    Emotional expressiveness: Cross-cultural differences in emotional display can reflect rules about context, relationships and social
    appropriateness. Avoid assuming that outward expression directly measures inner feeling.
    Schools: values and expectations: Schools transmit academic knowledge and cultural expectations about time, authority,
    competition, cooperation and appropriate conduct.
04Understanding and Explaining Culture

  Your faculty's syllabus specifies research ethics rather than the whole chapter. Use these principles when discussing fieldwork.

  Informed consent
  Meaning: Participants should understand the purpose, procedures, possible risks and voluntary nature of research before
  agreeing.
    Example 1: A researcher explains an interview project and asks permission before recording.
    Why it fits: The participant is informed about what participation involves.
    Example 2: A researcher explains that a participant may skip questions or withdraw.
    Why it fits: Consent remains voluntary and ongoing.

  Confidentiality and anonymity
  Meaning: Researchers protect personal information. Confidentiality means information is not disclosed improperly; anonymity
  means identity is not linked to responses.
    Example 1: A report uses pseudonyms for interviewees.
    Why it fits: Names are concealed to reduce identification.
    Example 2: Audio files are stored securely and identifying details are removed from transcripts.
    Why it fits: Private information is protected from unauthorized access.

  Do no harm
  Meaning: Researchers should minimize physical, social, emotional, reputational and economic risks to participants.
    Example 1: A researcher avoids publishing details that could expose a vulnerable participant.
    Why it fits: The researcher prevents foreseeable social or reputational harm.
    Example 2: An interviewer pauses when a topic causes distress and offers the option not to continue.
    Why it fits: Participant welfare takes priority over collecting data.

  Reciprocity and respect
  Meaning: Researchers should treat participants as partners, respect local knowledge and consider what communities receive
  from research.
    Example 1: A researcher shares a plain-language summary of findings with participants.
    Why it fits: Knowledge is returned in an accessible form.
    Example 2: A researcher schedules interviews around community responsibilities.
    Why it fits: The process respects participants' time and priorities.

  Ethical representation
  Meaning: Researchers should represent people accurately, avoid stereotypes and explain context and limitations.
    Example 1: A report includes diverse voices rather than describing a whole community through one interview.
    Why it fits: It avoids overgeneralization.
    Example 2: A researcher explains how their own position may shape interpretation.
    Why it fits: Reflexivity makes the researcher's influence visible.
    Fieldwork ethics checklist: Before fieldwork: permissions, consent plan, risk assessment. During fieldwork: voluntary participation,
    privacy, respect, safety and secure data. After fieldwork: careful representation, responsible storage and sharing results
    appropriately.
05Communication and Language
CHAPTER 5 — Communication and Language
  Scope in your syllabus: Sections 5.1, 5.2, 5.7, 5.8 and all boxes; include assigned accent video

  Communication includes verbal and nonverbal signals. Language is a symbolic system with shared conventions, and language
  use varies with context, identity and power.

  Communication
  Meaning: The exchange and interpretation of information through verbal, nonverbal and symbolic means.
    Example 1: A conversation uses words, tone and pauses.
    Why it fits: Meaning is communicated through spoken language and vocal cues.
    Example 2: A nod signals agreement in a particular setting.
    Why it fits: A gesture carries meaning through shared convention.

  Nonverbal communication
  Meaning: Communication through facial expression, gaze, gesture, posture, touch, distance and other cues.

    Example 1: Personal distance differs across social contexts.
    Why it fits: Physical space communicates comfort, status or relationship.
    Example 2: Eye contact can be interpreted differently across communities.
    Why it fits: Its meaning depends on cultural rules and setting.

  Language as a symbolic system
  Meaning: Language uses conventional sounds or signs to represent meanings and allows people to communicate beyond
  immediate situations.
    Example 1: The word 'tree' refers to a plant through a shared convention.
    Why it fits: The word is a symbol; its meaning is socially shared.
    Example 2: A sign-language gesture represents a concept.
    Why it fits: The gesture functions as a conventional symbol within a language system.

  Linguistic relativity
  Meaning: The language people use can influence habitual attention and categorization, without rigidly determining what they can
  think.
    Example 1: Languages categorize directions or spatial relations differently.
    Why it fits: Different linguistic systems can encourage different habitual ways of describing space.
    Example 2: Languages may encode kinship distinctions with different levels of detail.
    Why it fits: Vocabulary can make certain social distinctions more salient.

  Ethnography of speaking
  Meaning: Study of how language is used appropriately in social settings: who speaks, to whom, when, where, how and for what
  purpose.
    Example 1: A student uses a formal register when addressing a professor.
    Why it fits: Speech style reflects audience and social role.
    Example 2: A community uses special speech forms during ceremonies.
    Why it fits: The event has culturally specific rules for speaking.

  Accent and dialect
  Meaning: An accent concerns pronunciation patterns; a dialect can include pronunciation, vocabulary and grammar associated
  with a region or social group.
    Example 1: Speakers from two regions pronounce the same word differently.
    Why it fits: Pronunciation differences are accents.
    Example 2: Two regional varieties use different vocabulary and grammatical patterns.
    Why it fits: The variation extends beyond pronunciation and may be described as dialect variation.

  Language, identity and power
  Meaning: Language varieties can signal belonging and can be unfairly ranked by institutions or listeners.
    Example 1: A regional accent is wrongly treated as a sign of low intelligence.
    Why it fits: The judgment attaches social status to a speech variety rather than measuring ability.
    Example 2: A multilingual employee shifts language to communicate with different customers.
    Why it fits: Language choice helps manage identity, audience and access.

  Code-switching
  Meaning: Alternating between languages or language varieties depending on audience, topic, setting or identity.
    Example 1: A student speaks one language with family and another in class.
    Why it fits: Language choice responds to social context.
    Example 2: A bilingual worker shifts to a technical language when discussing a specialist task.
    Why it fits: The shift serves communicative purpose and audience needs.

  Language endangerment and revitalization
  Meaning: A language is endangered when transmission and everyday use decline; revitalization supports learning and use
  across generations.
    Example 1: A community starts children's language classes.
    Why it fits: The programme rebuilds intergenerational transmission.

06Getting Food
CHAPTER 6 — Getting Food
  Scope in your syllabus: Sections 6.1, 6.2 and all boxes; assigned videos on Hadza, foraging, pastoralism and Ladakh

  Food-getting systems are shaped by ecology, technology, labor, mobility, population, social organization and cultural knowledge.

  Foraging
  Meaning: Obtaining food from naturally available wild plants, animals and other resources rather than cultivating crops or raising
  domesticated herds as the main strategy.
    Example 1: Hadza communities in Tanzania have traditionally relied on hunting and gathering wild foods.
    Why it fits: Food is acquired from naturally available resources; the example also illustrates detailed environmental knowledge.
    Example 2: Urban residents gather edible wild plants where safe and permitted.
    Why it fits: The food is collected from uncultivated resources, although this is a limited contemporary practice.

  Foraging knowledge
  Meaning: Knowledge of species, seasons, habitats, animal behavior and safe food preparation that supports resource collection.
    Example 1: A forager identifies edible plants by season and habitat.
    Why it fits: This knowledge makes wild-resource use possible.
    Example 2: A hunter tracks animal movement and behavior.
    Why it fits: The knowledge guides the timing and location of hunting.

  Horticulture
  Meaning: Small-scale cultivation, often using relatively simple tools and lower-intensity land use, sometimes with shifting plots
  and fallow periods.
    Example 1: A household grows mixed crops in a garden plot.
    Why it fits: It is small-scale cultivation for food.
    Example 2: A community clears a plot, cultivates it and later allows it to lie fallow.
    Why it fits: The rotation and fallow pattern are characteristic of shifting cultivation systems.

  Intensive agriculture
  Meaning: Cultivation that uses substantial labor, technology or inputs to obtain high yields from a given area.
    Example 1: Irrigated rice farming with coordinated water and labor management.
    Why it fits: It requires intensive management and repeated cultivation.
    Example 2: Terrace cultivation on hillsides.
    Why it fits: Labor and constructed fields enable productive use of limited land.

  Pastoralism
  Meaning: A subsistence strategy centered on herding domesticated animals, often involving seasonal movement to find pasture
  and water.
    Example 1: Herders move livestock between seasonal grazing areas.
    Why it fits: Movement follows pasture and water availability.
    Example 2: A community depends on cattle, sheep or goats for milk, meat and other products.
    Why it fits: Livestock are central to livelihood and food production.

  Environmental adaptation
  Meaning: Adjusting livelihood strategies to climate, terrain, water, soil and resource availability.

    Example 1: Arid-region herders move animals to seasonal water and pasture.
    Why it fits: Mobility responds to uneven resource distribution.
    Example 2: Farmers in a wet region select crops suited to local rainfall.
    Why it fits: Crop choice reflects environmental conditions.

  Food-getting and social organization
  Meaning: The way food is obtained influences work roles, cooperation, mobility, settlement and resource sharing.
    Example 1: A fishing community coordinates boat crews and sharing.
    Why it fits: Food production depends on organized cooperation.
    Example 2: A pastoral group coordinates seasonal movement and herd care.

07Economic Systems
CHAPTER 7 — Economic Systems
  Scope in your syllabus: Sections 7.1 and 7.3 and all boxes

  Economic anthropology studies how people organize production, allocate resources and distribute goods and services. Economic
  behavior is embedded in social relationships and cultural values.

  Allocation of resources
  Meaning: The rules and practices that determine who can access or control land, water, labor and other resources.
    Example 1: A family owns and manages a plot of farmland.
    Why it fits: Access is allocated through recognized private property rights.
    Example 2: A village manages a common grazing area through shared rules.
    Why it fits: Access is allocated collectively through community institutions.

  Production
  Meaning: The organization of labor, technology and resources to create goods or services.
    Example 1: Farmers combine land, seed, tools and labor to grow crops.
    Why it fits: Inputs are organized to produce food.
    Example 2: A garment unit combines fabric, machines, workers and supervision.
    Why it fits: Labor and materials are transformed into clothing.

  Division of labor
  Meaning: The distribution of different tasks among people or groups.
    Example 1: In a farming household, some members prepare fields while others manage livestock.
    Why it fits: Tasks are allocated among household members.
    Example 2: A factory assigns cutting, stitching, quality checking and packing to different workers.
    Why it fits: Production is divided into specialized tasks.

  Distribution
  Meaning: The ways goods and services move from producers to other people.
    Example 1: A family shares harvested food with relatives.
    Why it fits: Goods move through social sharing.
    Example 2: A shop sells vegetables to customers for money.
    Why it fits: Goods are distributed through market exchange.

  Reciprocity
  Meaning: Exchange based on giving and receiving within social relationships, often with an expectation of return that may be
  delayed.
    Example 1: Friends exchange help during a move and return the favor later.
    Why it fits: The exchange is based on mutual obligation rather than a fixed price.
    Example 2: A household shares food with neighbors during a festival.
    Why it fits: Giving strengthens relationships and may be reciprocated later.

  Redistribution
  Meaning: Goods or resources are collected at a central point and then reallocated.
    Example 1: Government collects taxes and funds public services.
    Why it fits: Resources are centralized and then redistributed.
    Example 2: A community organization collects grain and distributes it to households during a shortage.
    Why it fits: A central body reallocates collected resources.

  Market exchange
  Meaning: Buying and selling goods or services, usually with money and prices.
    Example 1: A customer buys vegetables at a market.
    Why it fits: Price and payment organize the exchange.
    Example 2: A business sells clothing through an online store.
    Why it fits: A seller and buyer exchange a product for payment.

  Communal ownership
  Meaning: Resources are held or managed collectively under shared rules.
    Example 1: A village grazing ground is managed by community norms.
    Why it fits: The resource is collectively governed.
    Example 2: A cooperative owns equipment used by its members.
    Why it fits: Ownership and access are shared through membership.

  Remittances
  Meaning: Money sent by migrants to family members or communities in their place of origin.
    Example 1: A worker abroad sends part of their earnings to parents.
    Why it fits: Income crosses borders to support a household.
    Example 2: A migrant sends money to fund a sibling's education.
    Why it fits: The transfer links migration, family obligation and economic support.
    Communal ownership and economic outcomes: Shared ownership does not automatically cause success or failure. Outcomes
    depend on clear rules, monitoring, participation, incentives and ecological conditions.
    Working abroad and sending money home: Remittances support household spending, education, healthcare and local
    investment, but may also create dependence or change family roles.
    Deforestation and climate change: Economic activities such as logging, expansion of farms and infrastructure can contribute to
    forest loss. Examine the role of incentives, policy and local livelihoods.
08Social Stratification: Class, Ethnicity and Racism
CHAPTER 8 — Social Stratification: Class, Ethnicity and Racism
  Scope in your syllabus: Full chapter; link to intersectionality and inclusive workplaces

  Social stratification is structured inequality in access to resources, prestige and power. It is reproduced through institutions and
  social relationships, not only individual choices.

  Social stratification
  Meaning: A structured system of unequal access to valued resources, opportunities, status and power.
    Example 1: Unequal access to quality education across income groups.
    Why it fits: Resources and social position shape educational opportunities.
    Example 2: Unequal land ownership between households.
    Why it fits: Control over productive resources creates different life chances.

  Egalitarian society
  Meaning: A society with relatively limited permanent differences in wealth, prestige and power, though differences can still exist.
    Example 1: A small-scale community discourages hoarding and expects sharing.
    Why it fits: Norms limit accumulation and persistent wealth differences.
    Example 2: Leadership is temporary and based on situation-specific skill.
    Why it fits: Authority is less likely to become permanent and inherited.

  Rank society
  Meaning: A society where people or groups have unequal prestige or status, often inherited, while economic differences may be
  less extreme than in class societies.
    Example 1: A lineage holds higher ceremonial rank.
    Why it fits: Status is socially recognized and may be inherited.
    Example 2: Certain families have precedence in rituals.
    Why it fits: The society formally distinguishes status groups.

  Class society
  Meaning: A society where groups differ in economic resources and opportunities, with some possibility of movement between
  positions.
    Example 1: People occupy different income and wealth positions.
    Why it fits: Economic resources create class distinctions.
    Example 2: Access to elite education is easier for wealthy households.
    Why it fits: Class position influences opportunities and can reproduce inequality.

  Caste system
  Meaning: A relatively closed hereditary system of social categories, often maintained through rules about marriage, occupation
  and social interaction.
    Example 1: Caste membership is inherited by birth.
    Why it fits: Position is ascribed rather than selected freely.
    Example 2: Endogamy maintains boundaries between caste groups.
    Why it fits: Marriage rules help reproduce group divisions.

  Slavery
  Meaning: A system in which people are coerced and controlled as property or forced laborers, denied meaningful freedom.
    Example 1: Historical plantation systems forced enslaved people to work.
    Why it fits: Labor and freedom were controlled through coercion.
    Example 2: A historical system legally bound people to owners.
    Why it fits: People were treated as controlled property rather than free workers.

  Social mobility
  Meaning: Movement between social positions, especially changes in class or status.
    Example 1: A student from a low-income household enters a profession with higher income.
    Why it fits: The person's socioeconomic position changes upward.
    Example 2: A household loses its livelihood and moves into economic insecurity.
    Why it fits: The household experiences downward mobility.

  Race as a social category
  Meaning: Racial categories are created and given meaning by societies; human biological variation does not divide neatly into
  fixed discrete races.
    Example 1: Different societies classify the same ancestry or appearance differently.
    Why it fits: Classification depends on social systems and history.
    Example 2: Racial boundaries and labels change across historical periods.
    Why it fits: The category is socially constructed rather than a fixed biological map.

  Ethnicity

11Marital Residence and Kinship
CHAPTER 11 — Marital Residence and Kinship
  Scope in your syllabus: Specified definitions: neolocal, patrilocal, matrilocal residence; patrilineal and matrilineal descent

  Residence rules concern where a couple lives after marriage. Descent rules concern how people trace kinship and group
  membership.

  Neolocal residence
  Meaning: A married couple establishes a new residence separate from the households of both partners' parents.
    Example 1: A couple moves to a new apartment after marriage.
    Why it fits: They establish an independent household.
    Example 2: A couple relocates to another city for work and sets up a separate home.
    Why it fits: The residence is separate from both parental households.

  Patrilocal residence
  Meaning: A married couple lives with or near the husband's parents or patrilineal relatives.
    Example 1: A wife moves into the household of her husband's parents.
    Why it fits: Residence is organized around the husband's family.
    Example 2: A couple builds a home near the husband's brothers and parents.
    Why it fits: The couple resides near the husband's kin.

  Matrilocal residence
  Meaning: A married couple lives with or near the wife's parents or matrilineal relatives.
    Example 1: A husband moves to live with his wife's family.
    Why it fits: Residence is organized around the wife's family.
    Example 2: A couple settles near the wife's mother and siblings.
    Why it fits: The location is tied to the wife's kin.

  Patrilineal descent
  Meaning: Descent and group membership are traced through the male line, from father to children and onward.

    Example 1: A child belongs to the father's lineage.
    Why it fits: Lineage membership follows the paternal line.
    Example 2: Family property or clan membership passes through male ancestors.
    Why it fits: The descent principle is traced through men.

  Matrilineal descent
  Meaning: Descent and group membership are traced through the female line, from mother to children and onward.
    Example 1: A child belongs to the mother's lineage.
    Why it fits: Lineage membership follows the maternal line.
    Example 2: Clan identity is inherited through the mother's family.

16Health and Illness
CHAPTER 16 — Health and Illness
  Scope in your syllabus: Summary and review; health as biological and socio-cultural; textile workers; Balaghat; AI data
  workers; fitness trackers; Nari AI

  Health is not only biological. It is shaped by culture, environment, income, work, gender, institutions, access to care and ideas
  about the body.

  Biocultural approach to health
  Meaning: Understanding health through the interaction of biological processes and cultural, social, economic and environmental
  conditions.
    Example 1: Malnutrition is influenced by diet, income, food availability, sanitation and care practices.
    Why it fits: Biology interacts with social and material conditions.
    Example 2: Stress-related illness is influenced by physiology, work pressure and social support.
    Why it fits: Health outcomes emerge from interacting biological and social factors.

  Disease and illness
  Meaning: Disease usually refers to a biomedical condition; illness refers to the lived experience and social meaning of being
  unwell. The distinction can vary by context.
    Example 1: A clinician diagnoses diabetes using medical criteria.
    Why it fits: This is a disease category.
    Example 2: A person describes how diabetes affects family meals, identity and daily routine.
    Why it fits: This is the lived experience of illness.

  Medical anthropology
  Meaning: The study of how culture, society, economics and politics shape health, illness, healing and medical systems.
    Example 1: Researching why a community prefers a particular healing practice.
    Why it fits: The study examines cultural meanings and healthcare choices.
    Example 2: Studying barriers to maternal healthcare.
    Why it fits: The research connects institutions, gender, resources and health.

  Social determinants of health
  Meaning: Conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age that shape health opportunities and outcomes.
    Example 1: Unsafe housing increases exposure to illness.
    Why it fits: Living conditions affect health risks.
    Example 2: Low wages make nutritious food and healthcare harder to access.
    Why it fits: Economic position influences health opportunities.

  Structural violence
  Meaning: Social arrangements and institutions that systematically expose some groups to avoidable harm or restrict their life
  chances.
    Example 1: Workers face preventable illness because workplace protections are inadequate.
    Why it fits: Institutional conditions create risk rather than a single isolated act.
    Example 2: A remote community has poor access to healthcare due to long-standing infrastructure gaps.
    Why it fits: Structural conditions limit access to health resources.

  Occupational health
  Meaning: The study and prevention of health risks associated with work and working conditions.
    Example 1: Textile workers exposed to dust without adequate ventilation.
    Why it fits: Workplace exposure creates health risk.
    Example 2: Workers handling chemicals without training or protective systems.
    Why it fits: Work processes and safety conditions affect health.

  AI and health technology

  oversight and accessibility.

17Practicing and Applying Anthropology
CHAPTER 17 — Practicing and Applying Anthropology
  Scope in your syllabus: Full chapter — self-study

  Applied anthropology uses anthropological knowledge and methods in practical settings. It can work with communities,
  institutions, governments, NGOs and businesses.

  Applied anthropology
  Meaning: Using anthropological research and theory to understand or address practical issues.
    Example 1: A researcher studies why a water project is not being used and consults residents.
    Why it fits: The research applies cultural understanding to improve a programme.
    Example 2: A company observes customers using a product before redesigning it.
    Why it fits: Anthropological methods inform practical decisions.

  Ethnographic methods in applied settings
  Meaning: Observation, interviews, participation and contextual analysis help understand how people behave in real settings.
    Example 1: A researcher observes how nurses coordinate during a hospital shift.
    Why it fits: Observation reveals actual routines and constraints.
    Example 2: A researcher interviews garment workers about safety practices.
    Why it fits: Interviews provide workers' perspectives and lived experience.

  Participatory approach
  Meaning: A process that involves community members in defining problems, planning, implementing and evaluating
  interventions.
    Example 1: Residents help design a local sanitation programme.
    Why it fits: The community participates in decision-making.
    Example 2: Farmers help select and test an irrigation intervention.
    Why it fits: Local knowledge shapes the solution.

  Cultural competence and reflexivity
  Meaning: Cultural competence involves learning to work respectfully across differences; reflexivity means examining how the
  researcher's position influences the research.
    Example 1: A researcher adapts interview language to participants' preferred language.
    Why it fits: The process improves respectful communication.
    Example 2: A researcher records how their assumptions may have shaped observations.
    Why it fits: The researcher critically examines their own influence.

  Business anthropology
  Meaning: Applying anthropological methods to understand consumers, workers, organizations, products and markets.
    Example 1: Ethnographic research reveals how customers actually use kitchen appliances.
    Why it fits: Observation uncovers needs that surveys may miss.
    Example 2: A workplace study examines informal communication and team culture.
    Why it fits: It explains organizational behavior through everyday practices.

  Ethics in applied work
  Meaning: Applied researchers should protect participants, avoid harm, maintain confidentiality, communicate findings honestly
  and consider who benefits.
    Example 1: A consultant anonymizes employee interviews before reporting themes.
    Why it fits: Confidentiality protects participants.
    Example 2: A researcher tells a community how findings will be used and shares results.
    Why it fits: Transparency and reciprocity support ethical practice.

  EXAM ANSWER FRAMEWORK
  For a definition question: Define the concept accurately, explain its key feature and distinguish it from a related concept.

  For an example question: State the example clearly, identify the feature that makes it relevant, and explicitly write “This
  exemplifies ___ because…”.

  For a case-study question: Briefly describe the case, identify two or more anthropological concepts, apply each concept to
  evidence from the case, discuss power or context, and end with a balanced conclusion.

  For a critical-thinking question: Avoid one-sided answers. Identify benefits, harms, whose interests are served, whose voices
  are missing and what evidence would be needed.
   Example quality check: Use two distinct examples, not two versions of the same example. Explain the concept-specific feature
   in both. Do not treat a single film, news story or community as representative of every society.


  QUICK DEFINITIONS TO KEEP DISTINCT
  • Sex: biological sex characteristics. Gender: cultural and social meanings, roles and identities.

  • Intersex: variation in sex characteristics. Transgender: gender identity differs from sex assigned at birth.

  • Patrilineal: descent through father’s line. Patrilocal: residence with or near husband’s kin.

  • Matrilineal: descent through mother’s line. Matrilocal: residence with or near wife’s kin.

  • Neolocal: couple establishes a separate new residence.

  • Polyandry: one woman has multiple husbands. Polygyny: one man has multiple wives.

  • Negotiation: parties discuss directly. Mediation: third party facilitates. Adjudication: authorized body decides.

  • Race: socially constructed classification often linked to perceived physical traits. Ethnicity: identity linked to shared culture,
  ancestry, language or history.

  • Reciprocity: exchange through social obligation. Redistribution: central collection and reallocation. Market exchange: buying
  and selling, usually for money.

  • Ascribed status: assigned or inherited. Achieved status: acquired through action or accomplishment.
